Broadside Display — read me first

BoardKeeper's Desk

Putting it online (about three minutes, once)

  1. Go to app.netlify.com/drop.
  2. Drag this whole folder onto the page.
  3. It hands you a web address. That's your board. Share it.
  4. Make a free account when it offers, so you can come back and update the same address instead of getting a new one each time.

Free, no credit card, no command line.

If more than one person will publish, use GitHub Pages instead. Netlify's free plan has no team seats — you would all be sharing one login. A public GitHub repository allows unlimited collaborators for free, each with their own account. See Sharing this with several keepers below. Netlify Drop is still the fastest way to get something on screen today, and you can move later.

One thing that will bite you if nobody says it: open keeper.html from the web address, not by double-clicking the file on your computer. Browsers block local files from reading each other, so the Keeper's Desk can't assemble a complete export when it's opened straight off your disk.

Running a cycle

  1. Open the Keeper's Desk — that's keeper.html on your board's address.
  2. Drop in your scans and photos. The Prepare editor opens on the first one with the sheet already found — press Straighten to this, then Save and it moves to the next. Skip any you don't need to touch, Stop to leave the rest alone.
  3. For one photo holding several notices, use Cut slips out on the source to split it up first; you will be walked through preparing each piece when you close the cutter.
  4. Give each one a size, a kind, a headline and body text.
  5. Check the preview on the right — it shows exactly what the board will look like.
  6. Post this cycle. Slats slide out of the bottom, everything settles, the new ones load on top.
  7. Export the whole site, then drag that zip back onto Netlify. Done.

Sizes

  • Full — 8.5 × 11 portrait. Pinned to one slat and hangs over the one below. Special request only.
  • Half — 8.5 × 5.5 landscape. One whole column. The normal size.
  • Quarter — 4.25 × 5.5 portrait. Half a column.
  • Eighth — 4.25 × 2.75 landscape. Quarter of a column.

You never place anything by hand. Biggest goes centre and thins outward; overflow climbs to the slat above filling from the edges inward, which is what gives each cycle its downward chevron. Order the staging list if you want to influence what lands centre.

Bringing a whole batch in at once

Once submissions are coming in through a form, you don't type them out. On Tally's Submissions tab there are two downloads: Download CSV, and a batch download that gives you every uploaded file in one go. Take both, then drop them together onto the import box in the Keeper's Desk.

It reads the spreadsheet, works out which column is the headline and which is the body and so on, matches each response to its picture by filename, and shows you a table of what it found before anything is added. Then one button stages the lot.

  • It only guesses once. Correct any column it got wrong and the mapping is remembered, so every future import is drop-and-go.
  • Responses you've already imported are skipped. Download the whole CSV every time — it works out which rows are new. There's a Forget import history button if you ever need it to offer everything again.
  • Missing pictures are reported, not hidden. If a response mentions a file you haven't downloaded, that row is flagged missing rather than silently arriving blank.
  • Nothing is fetched over the internet. It reads the files off your disk, so there's no access token to expire and it works with the wifi off.

Anything the form didn't specify — usually the size — is left for you to set, with a sensible guess based on whether the picture is portrait or landscape.

Per-notice controls

  • Margin — how much breathing room the sheet keeps around its text. Drag it down to squeeze a long notice onto a small slip; nothing ever escapes the edge of the sheet, it just clips. Push it past 100% for an airier look.
  • Overflow warning — if a notice is being cut off, the row says so. Shrink the margin, trim the words, or move up a size. The full text always shows when a reader taps the slip, so a clipped notice is a look problem, not a lost-content problem.
  • Prepare… — reopens the corners, edges and tone editor on that picture. See below.
  • Turn — ⟲ ⟳ for quarter turns and a slider for a fine tilt. See below.
  • Duplicate — copies a notice, picture and all, right below the original.
  • Style — eight looks for any picture: Aged, Pen and ink, Engraving, Broadsheet, Painted, Watercolour, Woodblock, or leave it as photographed. The strength slider trades fine detail against paper grain — turn it down to keep the thinnest strokes, up to stop a grainy photo speckling.

Styles are never destructive. The Desk keeps the untouched original beside every styled picture, so you can change your mind or go back to the photograph at any time. Originals stay on your computer and are not published — visitors have no use for them and it would double the size of the site. The one consequence: if you pull a board another keeper published, you get their styled pictures but not their originals, so restyling those means starting from the styled version.

The drawing styles publish as PNG with a transparent background, so the ink sits on the board's parchment instead of on a white rectangle. There is a Style all pictures control in the staging toolbar for doing a whole batch in one go.

Preparing a picture

Every picture goes through this, however it arrives — dropped on the box, taken whole off a source, cut out of a sheet holding several notices, or brought in with a batch from your form. The editor opens on the first one straight away. You can always reopen it later with Prepare… on any notice.

  1. Pick the size, then the corners. The size box is the first thing in the editor — set it before you crop and everything else follows from it.
    Then put the four handles on the corners of the actual sheet. They do not have to make a rectangle, and that is the whole point: a notice photographed from an angle is a wonky quadrilateral, with the far edge shorter than the near one. Straighten to this maps that shape back onto a true rectangle, so what lands on the board is the square sheet it was before the camera got hold of it. The Desk finds the sheet and places the handles for you, so most notices need nothing but the button — Find it again re-detects if a handle goes astray, and ⟲ ⟳ turn a photo that came in sideways.
    Fit that size pads the result out to the chosen size's proportions with the sheet's own paper colour, so it fills its place on the board exactly. Nothing inside your selection is ever cropped away or stretched. If a lot of the slip comes out blank, that is the size being wrong for this sheet rather than the crop being wrong — the row tells you the percentage, and one click reopens the editor.
  2. Edges — for a thumb or a shadow that survived. Rub out paints to see-through with a soft edge; Put back restores from the picture you started with.
  3. Tone — settles the paper's colour against the board. At 0% you keep the scan exactly as photographed; at 100% its paper lands precisely on the board's colour. The ink stays ink either way — only the paper moves.

Save keeps it and moves to the next. Skip this one leaves that picture as it came in. Stop ends the run and leaves the remainder alone — so a batch of thirty is never thirty compulsory dialogs. Start over brings back the picture as imported.

Turning a notice

Every picture notice has a Turn row: ⟲ and ⟳ for quarter turns, a slider for a fine tilt of up to 45° either way, and square to undo the lot.

The angle is stored, not baked in. Each change re-derives the picture from the original, so turning something eight times leaves it exactly as sharp as turning it once, and going back to square gives you the original pixels rather than an approximation of them. A fine tilt opens up corners; those are filled with the picture's own paper colour, so the result stays a plain opaque rectangle.

Why the corners matter, and what happens underneath

A picture notice fills its sheet on the board edge to edge. There is no border of invented parchment around it, because a scan of a notice is the sheet — drawing another one behind it would put a fake sheet under a real one, and you would see the join.

Where the result's proportions do not exactly match the slot it lands in, a band of backing shows. That band is painted the scan's own paper colour, sampled when you prepared it, so it reads as more of the same sheet rather than as a cream frame.

Changing a notice's size afterwards

A picture is cropped to fit one size. Half and Eighth are the same shape as each other, so switching between those two changes nothing. Quarter and Full are the other way up, so switching to one of those leaves the picture keeping its own shape and sitting smaller inside the new slip.

That is a stopgap, not the finished article. The row says so, with a Re-crop to fit button beside it — one click reopens the editor with the new size already locked in, and Straighten to this trims the sheet to match. Nothing is ever stretched to fit, and nothing is cropped without you asking.

Working with the preview

The staged list scrolls inside its own box and the preview is pinned beside it, so the two stay together however many notices are waiting.

Tap any sheet in the preview to jump to it in the staging list. The list scrolls to that notice, outlines it, and puts the cursor in its headline — which is usually what you wanted when you spotted something wrong on the board.

The board's own colour

Under Settings there is a Board paper colour. Every sheet and every slat is built from that one colour, so changing it re-tunes the whole board at once.

Take it from the staged notices reads the paper tone out of the pictures you have staged and moves the board to meet them, rather than the other way round. It uses the median across all of them, so one badly lit scan in a batch of ten cannot drag the whole board off. That is usually the better direction: your notices are real paper photographed under real light, and it is easier to move the board than to argue with every scan.

The Tone tool inside Prepare does the opposite for a single awkward picture. Use whichever fits: the board colour for the general case, the Tone slider for the odd scan that is wildly off.

All of this is non-destructive. The picture as imported is kept beside the edited one, styles are always re-derived from the edited version rather than stacked on top of each other, and rotating turns both.

Where your work lives — read this bit

The Keeper's Desk stores everything in the browser on the computer you're using. It is not in the cloud. That means:

  • Clearing your browsing data will wipe it.
  • A second keeper on a different computer will not see your work until you publish and they pull.
  • The published board is only as current as your last export.

So: hit Save a backup file after each cycle and keep it somewhere shared. That file is how the job moves between people or machines — the other person hits Restore from a backup file.

Letting players submit

The board itself is just files, so it cannot receive anything. Submissions need a form service. tally.so is free with unlimited submissions and file uploads and needs no card — see the setup section below for exactly what to put on the form.

Avoid your host's own built-in form feature. On Netlify's free tier, going over the form allowance doesn't just stop the form — it takes every site on the account offline until you add a payment method.

Setting up the Tally form

Paste the form's address into Settings → Submission form address in the Keeper's Desk and a link appears on the board. Here is what the form should collect, in this order.

Fields to include

  1. In-world name — short text. This is what gets printed on the slip as the attribution.
  2. Player name or Discord handle — short text. Label it clearly as not shown on the board. You need a way to reach someone about their submission.
  3. Kind — dropdown: Notice, Rumour, Report, Bounty, Wanted, For Trade, Summons, Warning, Errata. This becomes the little label at the top of the slip, so a fixed list keeps the board looking consistent.
  4. Headline — short text, cap it around 50 characters.
  5. Body — long text. See the length guide below.
  6. Requested size — dropdown. Word the options the way the board does: Half (the normal size), Quarter, Eighth, and Full marked by request only. Say plainly that keepers may resize.
  7. Image — optional file upload, images only. Tally's free tier caps each file at 10 MB, which is far more than you need; ask for a photo or scan of the slip, not a phone screenshot of a photo.
  8. Which event is this for — dropdown of your event names, or a date.
  9. Acknowledgement — a required checkbox: "I understand this will be publicly visible on the internet, and I have not included anyone's real personal information." This is the single most useful field on the form.

How much text actually fits

These are guides for how the slip looks, not hard limits — the full text always appears when someone taps the slip, so nothing is ever lost, it just clips on the board face.

  • Full — headline plus roughly 600–800 characters.
  • Half — headline plus roughly 250–300.
  • Quarter — headline plus roughly 100–140.
  • Eighth — headline only. Treat it as a one-line shout.

Settings to turn on in Tally

  • Spam protection / captcha. It is a public form on the internet.
  • Email notification to whoever runs cycles, so submissions do not sit unseen.
  • Thank-you page pointing back at the board, with a line about when the next cycle goes up. It saves you the "did you get my thing" messages.
  • A close date per event, if you want a submission deadline.

When it is time to run a cycle, download the images from the Tally responses, drop them into the Keeper's Desk, and type the text across from the response table.

Editing a cycle you already posted

Every posted cycle has an Edit button. It reopens that cycle's notices exactly as you staged them — change wording, change sizes, withhold something, reorder it. Saving repacks that cycle and recomputes every cycle after it, so the board and the archive stay consistent. The banner tells you how many later cycles are affected.

The newest cycle also has Unpost, which takes it down and returns its notices to staging. Delete is on every cycle and removes it outright without returning anything — it is the blunt instrument for when something has gone wrong and you just want it gone. Either way, nothing reaches the public board until you export and publish again.

The staging list has Clear all if you want to start the batch over, and See it full size next to the preview opens the board exactly as visitors will see it, with a button to play the slat change.

Sharing this with several keepers

The Desk keeps its work in the browser it is opened in. With more than one keeper that is a clobbering risk, so the site now checks itself against what is actually published every time you open it, and tells you where you stand:

  • In step — you and the published board agree. Go ahead.
  • You have unpublished work — you have posted cycles but not uploaded them yet.
  • The published board is ahead — someone else has published since you last synced. Press Pull the published board before you touch anything, or you will overwrite them.
  • The two disagree — you and another keeper both changed things. Pulling adopts the published version and drops yours, so save a backup first if you need it.

Pulling brings down the cycles, the archive and every picture. If you try to export while the published board is ahead of you, it asks first. The published site is the shared source of truth — treat it the way you would a single paper ledger: sync, do your bit, publish, and tell the others.

The one rule that keeps this painless: only one keeper works at a time. Say in your staff channel when you start and when you have published. Nothing here can merge two people's simultaneous edits, and it will not pretend otherwise.

Giving them access to publish

This is where the choice of host matters, and Netlify Drop is not the right answer for a team.

  • Netlify's free plan has no team seats at all — multi-member access is a paid feature. Sharing one Netlify login between five people works, but everyone has full control of the account and you cannot take it back from one person without changing the password on everybody.
  • GitHub Pages is the better fit for a group. A public repository on the free plan allows unlimited collaborators, each with their own login, added and removed individually. Pages hosting on public repos is included.

To set that up: make a free GitHub account, create a public repository, upload the contents of this folder through the website (Add file → Upload files — no command line needed), then in Settings → Pages set the source to your main branch. You get an address like yourname.github.io/broadside.

Then Settings → Collaborators and invite each keeper by their GitHub username. After that, a keeper publishes by using Export just data and images in the Desk, unzipping it, and dragging the data and images folders into the repository through the website. That is a much smaller upload than the whole site, and it only touches the files that changed.

Two things you get free with this that are worth having: every publish is recorded, so you can see who changed what and roll back a bad cycle from GitHub's own history; and revoking a keeper is one click on the collaborators page, with no shared password to rotate.

The keeper password on the Desk is a separate thing and does not control publishing — see that section above.

The keeper password

Settings has a password field. Set one and the Keeper's Desk asks for it before it opens.

Be clear-eyed about what this is. The whole site is static files, so the check happens in the visitor's own browser. Someone who knows how to open developer tools can walk straight past it. It stops a curious player poking at an obvious address; it does not stop anyone determined.

That is fine, because there is nothing behind the gate worth stealing. The board data is public anyway, and the Desk only edits a copy inside that person's own browser. To change what the world sees, they would need to log into your host and upload a new zip — and the password has nothing to do with that. Use a short phrase you can tell your staff over the table.

Withholding versus deleting

Withhold keeps something in your working copy but never publishes it — for things that are merely off-tone. Delete destroys the image outright. Use Delete for anything that must not exist anywhere: real personal details, anything unlawful. It's gone from the published site the next time you export.

If something looks wrong

  • Board says it can't load data — you opened the file locally instead of from the web address.
  • Images missing after publishing — the export didn't include them; re-export from the hosted Keeper's Desk.
  • A cycle went out wrong — Undo on the most recent cycle puts its notices back in staging.
  • Nothing animates — the slide only plays for a visitor who saw an older cycle. Use Replay the last change.
